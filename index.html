\n CASE #001\n MAPPED 1539 – 1997\n
\n

Isla Bermeja

\n

Gulf of Mexico // 100 nautical miles off Yucatán

\n

\n For over 400 years, Spanish cartographers and international naval treaties plotted a coral-red speck northwest of the Yucatán Peninsula. In the late 1990s, when Mexico and the United States negotiated deep-sea oil rights in the "Doughnut Hole," Isla Bermeja should have pushed Mexico's maritime border outward by billions of barrels of crude.\n

\n
\n The Discovery: In 1997, the Mexican Navy dispatched an expedition to survey Bermeja. When their vessels reached the exact coordinates, they found nothing but deep, uninterrupted ocean. A second bathymetric sweep in 2009 confirmed water depth of 1,472 feet without a trace of a submerged reef.\n

\n CASE #002\n MAPPED 1325 – 1865\n

Hy-Brasil

\n

North Atlantic // West of Galway Bay, Ireland

\n

\n Long before the South American nation took its name, European mariners plotted a perfectly circular island off the Irish coast divided by a single straight river. Celtic folklore claimed it was cloaked in perpetual sea fog and only revealed itself once every seven years.\n

\n

\n In 1480 and 1481, Bristol merchants funded multiple armed voyages to locate it. In 1674, Captain John Nisbet claimed his crew anchored there after fog parted, reporting that it was inhabited by large black rabbits and an elderly sorcerer living in a stone castle.\n

\n CASE #003\n MAPPED 1798 – 1889\n

The Mountains of Kong

\n

West Africa // Supposed ridge from Guinea to Central Africa

\n

\n In 1798, English cartographer James Rennell synthesized explorer Mungo Park's field notes and drew a massive, jagged mountain range spanning thousands of miles across Africa. For nearly a century, every major atlas on Earth depicted the Kong Range as an impassable alpine barrier.\n

\n

\n It dictated European colonial strategy and trade routes until French explorer Louis-Gustave Binger marched straight across the supposed crest in 1888, finding only gentle rolling hills.\n

Sandy Island (Île de Sable)

\n

Coral Sea // Between Australia & New Caledonia

\n

\n First reported in 1876 by the whaling ship Velocity, this 15-mile sliver survived over a century on British Admiralty charts, modern world atlases, and digitized databases like Google Earth, Google Maps, and the CIA World Factbook.\n

\n
\n The Digital Erasure: In November 2012, Australian scientists aboard RV Southern Surveyor noticed navigational discrepancies between bathymetric maps and satellite databases. They diverted course to survey Sandy Island directly and found over 4,600 feet (1,400 meters) of unbroken open ocean.\n

\n CASE #005\n MAPPED 1906 – 1914\n
\n

Crocker Land

\n

Arctic Ocean // North of Ellesmere Island, Nunavut

\n

\n In 1906, celebrated polar explorer Robert Peary claimed he spotted snow-capped peaks rising high above the Arctic pack ice, naming the subcontinent "Crocker Land" after his financial patron George Crocker. Believing it held the key to Northern Hemisphere meteorology and sovereign territory, the American Museum of Natural History launched a full-scale expedition in 1913 under Donald MacMillan.\n

\n
\n The Optical Mirage: In April 1914, after dog-sledding over 125 miles across perilous shifting sea ice, MacMillan and his Inuit guide Piugaattoq reached the mapped location. Looking through binoculars, MacMillan saw vast mountains and valleys — until the sun shifted and the entire landmass dissolved into the horizon. It was a classic Arctic Fata Morgana atmospheric temperature inversion.\n

\n CASE #006\n MAPPED 1558 – 1860s\n
\n

Frisland (Frislandia)

\n

North Atlantic // South of Iceland / East of Greenland

\n

\n In 1558, Venetian nobleman Nicolò Zeno published a map and travel letters claiming his ancestors had discovered a civilized northern empire named Frisland in the 1390s, complete with fortified cities and thriving ports. Master mapmakers of the Renaissance — including Gerardus Mercator and Abraham Ortelius — copied Frisland onto the definitive atlases of Europe.\n

\n
\n The Royal Sovereign Claim: The phantom island was so widely accepted that in 1578, English explorer Martin Frobisher mistook the southern coast of Greenland for Frisland. He landed, planted the royal standard, and claimed the entire phantom landmass for Queen Elizabeth I under the name "West England."\n

\n CASE #007\n MAPPED 1762 – 1870s\n
\n

The Aurora Islands

\n

South Atlantic // Between Falkland Islands & South Georgia

\n

\n First reported in 1762 by the Spanish merchant vessel Aurora, this trio of jagged, snow-capped peaks was considered so geostrategically vital that the Royal Spanish Navy dispatched the corvette Atrevida in 1794 to conduct a formal hydrographic survey, meticulously mapping their coastlines, anchorages, and mountain summits.\n

\n
\n The Phantom Sweep: Despite the official Spanish naval coordinates, dozens of subsequent naval captains found only open ocean. By the 1870s, cartographers concluded the surveyors had been deceived by massive Antarctic tabular icebergs encrusted with dark volcanic ash, anchored temporarily on the shallow Shag Rocks shoal.\n

Saxemberg Island

\n

South Atlantic // ~30°45′S 19°30′W (Midway to Tristan da Cunha)

\n

\n In 1670, Dutch captain John Lindesz Lindeman reported sighting a lush, low island dominated by a central peak in the remote South Atlantic. For over two centuries, navigators across Europe plotted Saxemberg as a crucial potential resupply harbor on the grueling passage between the Americas and the Cape of Good Hope.\n

\n
\n The 4-Hour Observation: In 1804, American Captain Galloway of the ship Fanny swore under logbook oath that he sailed within sight of Saxemberg for four continuous hours, recording distinct sandy beaches and tree lines. However, repeated dedicated sweeps by famed navigator Matthew Flinders and HMS Barracouta revealed an unbroken ocean floor over 18,000 feet deep.\n

Rupes Nigra (The Black Rock)

\n

Geographic North Pole // Exact Arctic Center

\n

\n In 1569, master Flemish cartographer Gerardus Mercator published his legendary world map depicting a towering, 33-mile-wide mountain of magnetic lodestone standing at the exact North Pole. Surrounding this jet-black basalt titan were four massive whirlpool channels that continuously siphoned the northern seas straight into the inner mantle of the Earth.\n

\n
\n The Navigation Solution: Derived from the lost 14th-century travelogue Inventio Fortunata, Mercator invented the mountain to solve a critical puzzle that baffled Renaissance navigators: why iron compass needles pointed north anywhere on Earth.\n

\n CASE #010\n MAPPED 1367 – 1906\n

Mayda (Isle of Mam)

\n

North Atlantic // Southwest of Ireland (~46°24′N 37°18′W)

\n

\n Shaped like a distinct hook or crescent moon, Mayda first appeared in 1367 on the famous nautical chart of the Pizigani brothers, accompanied by Latin warnings of deadly dragons and sea sirens. Over the following five centuries, the crescent isle refused to disappear, hopping between Spanish, Portuguese, and British navigational charts.\n

\n
\n The 539-Year Longevity: Mayda was so deeply entrenched in international maritime tradition that it survived the Age of Discovery intact. Remarkably, it was still published on official navigation charts published by the United States Hydrographic Office as late as 1906, before modern telegraph cable soundings proved the seabed lay beneath 14,000 feet of open Atlantic.\n

Pepys Island

\n

South Atlantic // ~47°00′S 64°00′W (~230 miles north of Falklands)

\n

\n In December 1683, English buccaneer William Cowley aboard the privateer ship Revenge encountered an uncharted, forested coastline in the tempestuous South Atlantic. Seeking Royal favor and pardons for his crew's piracy, Cowley published an account in 1684 naming the newly sighted landmass "Pepys Island" in honor of Samuel Pepys, then Secretary to the Admiralty.\n

\n
\n The Admiralty Hunt: British admirals coveted Pepys Island as a strategic naval staging post to command the passage around Cape Horn. In 1764, the British Admiralty dispatched Commodore John Byron aboard HMS Dolphin specifically to locate and fortify it. Byron crisscrossed the latitude and found nothing. Capt. James Cook and HMS Beagle captain Robert FitzRoy later demonstrated Cowley had simply miscalculated the coordinates of the northern Falkland Islands.\n

Dougherty Island

\n

South Pacific Ocean // ~59°20′S 120°20′W (Midway Cape Horn & New Zealand)

\n

\n In 1841, Captain Dougherty aboard the English whaler James Stewart sighted a formidable 5-mile-long island bluff covered in snow and surrounded by pack ice in the roaring southern latitudes between New Zealand and Cape Horn. Eighteen years later in 1859, Captain Keates aboard the Louise confirmed its existence, describing high, dark bluffs with a round hill at the northeastern end.\n

\n
\n The Polar Hunt: Dougherty Island remained a crucial navigational landmark on British Admiralty charts for nearly a century. Famous Antarctic expeditions — including Captain Robert Falcon Scott on RRS Discovery (1904), Jean-Baptiste Charcot on Pourquoi-Pas? (1909), and Captain John King Davis on the Nimrod (1909) — sailed directly through the charted coordinates without catching sight of land. It was finally purged from Admiralty charts in 1937.\n

\n CASE #013\n MAPPED 1821 – 1909\n
\n

Emerald Island

\n

Southern Ocean // ~57°30′S 162°12′E (South of Macquarie Island)

\n

\n In December 1821, Captain William Lisle of the British sealing vessel Emerald recorded a mountainous, remarkably green island rising out of the Antarctic squalls south of Macquarie Island. Named after his ship, the purported green jewel in the freezing southern sea spurred a century of maritime controversy.\n

\n
\n The Polar Searches: In 1840, the United States Exploring Expedition under Commodore Charles Wilkes reached the charted position, recording 20-foot ocean swells and no trace of land. Later that same year, Royal Navy polar pioneer Sir James Clark Ross sailed directly over the location, finding sea depths exceeding 1,200 fathoms. Expeditions by Ernest Shackleton on the Nimrod (1909) and Roald Amundsen on the Fram (1910) definitively confirmed the green peaks were an optical illusion produced by sunlight refracting off ice cliffs through heavy fog.\n

\n CASE #014\n MAPPED 1528 – 1973\n
\n

Los Jardines (The Gardens)

\n

North Pacific Ocean // ~21°30′N 153°00′E (East of Mariana Islands)

\n

\n In May 1528, Spanish explorer Álvaro de Saavedra Cerón crossed the Pacific from Mexico toward the Spice Islands. On May 28, his crew encountered two lush, vegetation-covered islands inhabited by friendly islanders who offered water and coconuts. Saavedra plotted their position and named them Los Jardines (The Gardens).\n

\n

\n Though absent from some 17th-century maps, the phantom pair re-emerged with authority in 1788 when Captain John Marshall aboard the British transport ship Scarborough reported sighting two green islands at virtually the exact coordinates, re-confirming their status on international hydrographic charts.\n

\n
\n The 445-Year Purge: Los Jardines remained on official maritime charts into the late 20th century. During World War II, the US Navy conducted aerial reconnaissance sweeps to locate the islands for potential runway staging, finding only open Pacific waves. In 1973, after scientific research vessels confirmed seabed depths exceeding 16,000 feet (5,000 meters), the International Hydrographic Organization formally purged Los Jardines from global charts.\n

\n CASE #015\n MAPPED 1825 – 1920s\n
\n

Byers Island

\n

North Pacific Ocean // ~28°32′N 177°04′E (West of Midway Atoll)

\n

\n In July 1825, American sealing captain Benjamin Morrell aboard the schooner Tartar claimed to have discovered a moderately elevated, moderately wooded island approximately 4 miles in circumference in the central North Pacific. Morrell named the landmass "Byers Island" in honor of his New York shipowner, James Byers, and logged extensive details of abundant fresh water, sea turtles, and rich seal rookeries.\n

\n

\n Because Morrell was known for publishing sensationalized travel memoirs, maritime authorities treated his coordinates with suspicion. Yet because of the strategic value of coaling and emergency harbors in the mid-Pacific trade lanes, Byers Island was officially incorporated onto charts of the United States Navy Hydrographic Office and British Admiralty maps for nearly a century.\n

\n
\n The Naval Reconnaissance Sweeps: In 1857, the USS Vincennes was dispatched under Commander John Rodgers to confirm Byers Island for naval use; sounding leads reached endless depths without touching bottom. In 1867, Captain William Reynolds of the USS Lackawanna scoured the region across a 50-nautical-mile grid, followed by British survey ship HMS Alert in 1881. All recorded clear horizon and abyss depths. The island was finally struck from official US Navy hydrographic charts in the 1920s.\n

\n CASE #016\n MAPPED 1569 – 1630s\n
\n

Groclant

\n

Arctic North Atlantic // ~65°00′N 60°00′W (West of Greenland / Davis Strait)

\n

\n In 1569, legendary cartographer Gerardus Mercator published his seminal world chart depicting a sprawling, mountainous subcontinent positioned west of Greenland. Mercator labeled it Groclant, accompanied by detailed inlets, capes, and settlements. A year later, Abraham Ortelius placed Groclant on his renowned 1570 atlas Theatrum Orbis Terrarum, cementing its place in Renaissance geography.\n

\n

\n Historians believe the phantom landmass originated from corrupted Norse navigational logs and phonetic distortion of Greenland itself (Grønland), combined with early sightings of Baffin Island's glacier-draped coastlines.\n

\n
\n The Northwest Passage Quests: Groclant served as a primary waypoint for Elizabethan voyagers searching for a navigable Northwest Passage to Asia. Navigators Martin Frobisher (1576–1578), John Davis (1585), and William Baffin (1616) repeatedly probed the icy straits, progressively filling the charts with Baffin Bay and proving Mercator's Arctic continent was nothing more than open frozen sea.\n

\n CASE #017\n MAPPED 1858 – 1937\n
\n

Sarah Ann Island

\n

Equatorial Pacific Ocean // ~04°00′N 154°22′W (Northwest of Christmas Island)

\n

\n In 1858, an American guano vessel reported discovering a low-lying coral atoll in the central equatorial Pacific, christening it "Sarah Ann Island." Under the Guano Islands Act of 1856, the United States formally claimed sovereign federal jurisdiction over the island, officially cataloging it with the Department of State and the US Navy Hydrographic Office.\n

\n

\n For nearly eight decades, Sarah Ann Island sat quietly on Pacific nautical charts as an American territorial possession. But in 1937, international astronomers realized that the total solar eclipse of June 8, 1937 — the longest duration eclipse in over a millennium — would cross precisely over Sarah Ann's charted coordinates.\n

\n
\n The Eclipse Search Flotilla: The US Navy dispatched the tender USS Avocet and scientific observation vessels carrying telescopes to erect a ground observation observatory on the island. After extensive aerial reconnaissance and hydrographic sweeps across hundreds of square miles, navigators found nothing but unbroken ocean over 15,000 feet deep. Astronomers were forced to divert to Canton Island, and the US Navy promptly struck Sarah Ann from the federal registry.\n

\n CASE #018\n MAPPED 1825 – 1898\n
\n

Thompson Island

\n

Sub-Antarctic South Atlantic // ~54°26′S 03°24′E (~45 miles NE of Bouvet Island)

\n

\n On December 13, 1825, Captain George Norris of the Enderby & Sons sealing vessel Sprightly sighted an uncharted volcanic island rising out of the howling Sub-Antarctic gale, claiming it for King George IV and naming it "Thompson Island" after his investor George Thompson. Norris described a bleak, steaming expanse of black basalt, basalt pillars named the "Chimnies," and cliffs crowded with seal colonies.\n

\n

\n The island was subsequently spotted and confirmed in 1893 by Captain Joseph Fuller aboard the whaler Francis Allyn. Thompson Island was formally adopted onto British Admiralty charts and South Atlantic shipping routes as a hazardous navigation danger.\n

\n
\n The Cataclysmic Disappearance: In 1898, the German Deep Sea Expedition aboard the Valdivia under Carl Chun arrived to survey Thompson Island for a high-latitude meteorological station. Arriving at the exact coordinates, they found ocean depths exceeding 1,000 fathoms with zero trace of land. Modern volcanologists hypothesize that Thompson Island was an unstable volcanic cone that suffered a catastrophic submarine caldera collapse between 1893 and 1898, obliterating itself below sea level.\n
\n

\n CASE #019\n MAPPED 1508 – mid-1600s\n
\n

Isle of Demons (Isle des Démons)

\n

North Atlantic // ~50°45′N 55°30′W (Strait of Belle Isle, Newfoundland)

\n

\n First mapped in 1508 by cartographer Johannes Ruysch on his celebrated world chart Universalior Cogniti Orbis Tabula, the Isle of Demons was depicted as a jagged, fog-drenched pair of islands off the coast of Newfoundland. European sailors believed the island was populated by winged fiends, howling spirits, and predatory beasts that assaulted any ship daring to drop anchor.\n

\n

\n In 1542, French nobleman and colonial leader Jean-François de Roberval marooned his young relative Marguerite de La Rocque on the island along with her lover and maidservant as punishment for an illicit affair. Marguerite survived on the island for over two years in a cave, enduring freezing blizzards, attacks by wild bears, and the shrieks of sea gales that sounded like demonic hordes, before being rescued in 1544 by Basque fishermen.\n

\n
\n The Demystification: Sixteenth-century mapmakers Giacomo Gastaldi (1556) and André Thevet (1558) documented the island with elaborate woodcut illustrations of horned creatures. As regular Newfoundland cod fisheries expanded, navigators realized the terrifying howling was produced by dense nesting colonies of breeding shearwaters and puffins echoing against sea cliffs in heavy fog. Cartographers gradually removed the supernatural island from North American charts in the mid-17th century.\n
