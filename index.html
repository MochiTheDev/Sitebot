The Antikythera Mechanism

Exploring the pinnacle of Hellenistic engineering and the world's oldest known astronomical computer.

Bronze Clockwork of Antiquity

Recovered in 1901 from a Roman-era shipwreck off the Greek island of Antikythera, this corroded lump of bronze concealed a complex arrangement of over thirty meshing bronze gear wheels. It predicted solar and lunar eclipses, tracked the zodiacal path of the Sun and Moon, modeled lunar anomaly (the eccentric orbit of the Moon), and even synchronized the four-year cycle of the Panhellenic Games.

Discovery

The 1901 Shipwreck

Sponge divers sheltering from a storm discovered the wreck at a depth of 45 meters, unveiling statues, coins, and a fragmented shoebox-sized mechanism that defied conventional historical timelines.

Kinematics

Differential & Epicyclic Gearing

Centuries before mechanical clocks emerged in Renaissance Europe, the device used a pin-and-slot mechanism to emulate Kepler's second law approximation via Hipparchus's lunar theories.

Cycles

The Saros & Metonic Dials

The rear spirals displayed the 235-month Metonic calendar and the 223-month Saros eclipse cycle, calculating the exact month, hour, and celestial characteristics of expected eclipses.

Did you know? Modern micro-focus X-ray computed tomography revealed thousands of Greek inscription characters inside the fragments, serving as a built-in user manual for operating the device.
